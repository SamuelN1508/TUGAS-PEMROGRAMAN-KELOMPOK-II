Abstract—The grading of open-ended questions in educational 
settings is a labour-intensive and subjective process, often leading to 
inconsistencies and biases in scoring. Conventional automated and 
manual grading systems have faced challenges in accurately 
interpreting the complexity and variability of student responses. This 
study addressed these challenges by developing a transformer-based 
scoring approach that leverages advanced artificial intelligence and 
semantic similarity techniques to provide consistent, accurate, and 
efficient grading of open-ended questions. The study evaluated the 
performance of MiniLM, BERT, RoBERTa, and SBERT in grading 
short-answer questions based on correlation, agreement, semantic 
sensitivity, and computational efficiency. Results indicate that 
SBERT exhibits the highest semantic sensitivity across different 
questions (Q1: 0.726, Q2: 0.881, Q3: 0.798) and strong correlation 
with MiniLM (Pearson: 0.909, Spearman: 0.908). MiniLM provides 
a competitive balance between efficiency and accuracy, processing 
responses in 80.06 seconds compared to RoBERTa’s 1359.64 
seconds, making MiniLM 16.98 times faster and substantially more 
efficient for grading large volumes of responses in real-time or large-
scale grading scenarios. Score distribution analysis revealed that 
SBERT and MiniLM produce similar grading patterns, while 
RoBERTa demonstrates the highest variance. BERT and RoBERTa 
demonstrate near-random agreement (-0.020), as reflected in 
Cohen’s Kappa scores which indicates inconsistencies. These 
findings emphasize the importance of model selection in automated 
grading, with SBERT emerging as the most robust in capturing 
semantic similarity. The proposed approach demonstrates the 
potential to alleviate educators' grading burden with transformer-
based scoring while enhancing the fairness and reliability of student 
evaluations.

I. INTRODUCTION 
The assessment of student performance is a very important 
component of the educational process, as it provides valuable 
insights into learning outcomes, knowledge retention, and 
conceptual understanding. Traditional assessment methods, 
particularly those involving open-ended questions, present 
significant challenges for human evaluators in terms of 
scalability, subjectivity, and grading consistency [1], [2]. These 
challenges can result in inconsistencies and a lack of fairness in 
evaluations [3], [4]. Unlike multiple-choice or fill-in-the-blank 
assessments, which can be graded efficiently using rule-based 
algorithms, evaluating open-ended responses requires deeper 
semantic understanding, making manual grading both time-
consuming, labour-intensive and prone to inconsistencies [5], 
[6], [7].

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and 
natural language processing (NLP) have revolutionized various 
domains [8], [9], [10], including educational assessment [11], 
[12], [13]. AI-driven automated grading systems have emerged 
as a promising solution to alleviate the burdens of manual 
grading while enhancing accuracy, efficiency, and fairness 
[14], [15]. Among AI-based methods, transformer-based 
models, such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations 
from Transformers) and Sentence-BERT (SBERT), have 
demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in semantic text 
similarity and comprehension tasks [16], [17], [18], [19]. These 
models leverage deep learning techniques to capture intricate 
language structures, enabling them to assess responses based on 
semantic similarity rather than mere keyword matching. 
Despite their potential, selecting the most suitable transformer 
model for automated grading remains a critical challenge, as 
different models exhibit varying levels of accuracy, agreement, 
and computational efficiency. 
The application of transformer-based models in grading 
open-ended questions holds significant promise. These models 
can potentially address the limitations of traditional and earlier 
automated grading systems by offering a more sophisticated 
analysis of student responses by capturing contextual meaning, 
paraphrased expressions, and subtle linguistic variations. They 
can analyse students' written responses, evaluate the content 
and structure, and generate scores that align with human 
evaluators' standards. Despite these potential benefits, the 
integration of AI in grading open-ended questions presents 
several challenges such as understanding context, handling 
ambiguity, lack of domain specific knowledge and deployment 
[20], [21]. Ensuring the reliability, fairness, and transparency of 
AI-driven grading systems is paramount. 
This study aims to evaluate the performance of several 
transformer-based models: MiniLM, BERT, RoBERTa, and 
SBERT in the grading of short-answer questions. The models 
are assessed based on their correlation in model scores, 
agreement metrics, semantic sensitivity, and computational 
efficiency. Specifically, the study seeks to determine which 
model offers the best balance between accuracy and processing 
speed while maintaining reliable grading patterns.  
By addressing the challenges of inconsistency and 
subjectivity in manual grading, this research highlights the 
potential of transformer-based scoring approaches to reduce 
grading time, improve fairness and reliability of student 
evaluations. Furthermore, this study provides empirical 
evidence on the reliability and effectiveness of transformer-
based models for automated evaluation, paving the way for 
broader adoption in computer-assisted learning, remote 
education, and standardized testing. 
II. RELATED WORKS 
Numerous methodologies have been proposed for 
automated short-answer grading systems. This section reviews 
relevant studies that focus on the automatic evaluation of short-
answer responses. 
The study by [22] developed a framework for automatic 
grading of short-answers. The framework combined semantic 
features based on BERT and lexical features using TF-IDF 
(Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) for improved 
accuracy in grading. A unique part of the framework is the 
support for non-English dataset. The experimental results 
showed that the framework named GradeAid achieved a 
competitive performance with a root mean squared error 
(RMSE) of 0.25. 
The study by [23] focused on the automatic grading of 
responses to questions using semantic similarity in a low 
resource language; Arabic. Local and hybrid local-global 
weighting technique was applied to a dataset containing 2,133 
model-student answer pairs. The evaluation results revealed 
that the hybrid local-global weighting achieved a superior 
performance with a F1-score of 82.82% and RMSE of 0.798. 
In a bid to autonomously and accurately evaluate short-
answer questions [24], hybridized multiple tokenizers 
(DistilBERT, MPNet, XLNet and RoBERTa) with BERT. The 
evaluation of these models revealed an accuracy up to 99.6%. 
However, the generalizability across diverse subject is yet to be 
established. 
A study by [25] introduced GRAD-AI, an AI-powered 
grading tool for computer programming assignments that 
combines automation with teacher involvement. Using 
techniques like the Halstead Complexity Measure, TF-IDF, 
Abstract Syntax Tree processing, and K-means clustering, it 
ensures accurate, unbiased, and timely feedback. Experimental 
results showed its effectiveness in delivering precise 
assessments and real-time feedback, fostering interactive 
learning. 
Reference [26] focused on the assessment of Ai model’s 
performance on open-ended medical questions. In the study, 21 
open-ended certification examination questions on head and 
neck were used to test ChatGPT’s performance. The responses 
from the language model were evaluated based on concordance, 
validity, safety and competency (CVSC). After three trials 
ChatGPT achieved mean passing score of 75% and performed 
better when guided with prompts. High concordance (92.06%) 
and satisfactory validity were also recorded. However, the 
study noted that ChatGPT sometimes provided partially correct 
responses and generated misleading information. In a similar 
study [27] an investigation into the effectiveness of fine-tuned 
GPT-3.5 for the grading of students responses to science 
education questions was carried out in a bid to address the 
limitation of direct pre-trained model usage due to differences 
in student language pattern and the need for contextual 
understanding. The performance of the fine-tuned GPT-3.5 was 
compared to fine-tuned BERT. The result revealed that GPT3.5 
achieved a superior performance over BERT with a mean 
scoring accuracy of 9.1% across tasks. 
The feasibility of GPT and Llama for automatic grading of 
short-answer was investigated in this study [28]. The 
experimental setup included zero-shot, few-shot, fine-tuned 
variants of GPT and Llama, and supervised (BERT and SVM)

models. The results revealed that the LLMs performance for

The LLMs performance matched the supervised models after

The study by [30] hybridized word level text preprocessing 
algorithm, artificial neural network (ANN), text similarity 
algorithm and heuristic decision rules for scoring students’ 
answer in Polish. The proposed approach achieved a high 
grading accuracy. However, the false negatives indicate the 
need for further refinements. 
It can be observed from the related work that existing 
studies on automated short-answer grading have made 
significant strides but still present notable gaps. Many 
frameworks, such as GradeAid [22] and GRAD-AI [25], 
integrate AI-driven approaches but rely heavily on traditional 
lexical features such as TF-IDF or structural analysis (Abstract 
Syntax Trees), limiting their ability to capture deep semantic 
meaning. While studies leveraging transformer models, such as 
those by [24] and [28], highlight their high accuracy, concerns 
remain about their generalizability across diverse subjects, 
languages, and question types. Additionally, research into large 
language models (LLMs) like GPT-3.5 and Llama [27], [28]  
demonstrates that their zero-shot and few-shot performance is 
inadequate for grading, requiring extensive fine-tuning for 
effective assessment. Furthermore, comparative analyses of 
different transformer-based models for short-answer grading 
are scarce, with most studies focusing on a single model rather 
than evaluating multiple architectures in terms of accuracy, 
efficiency, and agreement. 
Given these limitations, this study justifies its contributions 
by conducting a comparative analysis of MiniLM, BERT, 
RoBERTa, and SBERT to determine the most effective 
transformer-based model for short-answer grading. Unlike 
previous studies that focus on either lexical features or a single 
transformer model, this research evaluates multiple models 
across 
critical metrics, including 
semantic 
sensitivity, 
correlation, agreement, and computational efficiency. By doing 
so, it provides deeper insights into model selection for 
automated grading, identifying an optimal balance between 
accuracy and efficiency. 
III. METHODOLOGY 
A. Data Collection 
The dataset used in this study comprises responses from 225 
students offering COS101: Introduction to Computing Science 
at the Federal University of Health Sciences, Otukpo, Benue 
State, Nigeria [31]. The students answered three open-ended 
questions: (Q1) What was the significance of Ada Lovelace's 
work on the Analytical Engine? (Q2) What contribution did 
John von Neumann make to computer architecture? (Q3) What 
was the contribution of John Presper Eckert and John W. 
Mauchly to computer development? The data was collected via 
Google Forms and stored in a CSV file containing four columns: 
Matric Number, Q1, Q2, and Q3. The columns Q1, Q2, and Q3 
holds the students’ responses to each of the questions asked.  
Each response was evaluated against predefined correct answers 
to determine semantic similarity and assign scores. 
B. Sentence Embedding Models  
Four transformer-based sentence embedding models were 
employed to measure semantic similarity between student 
responses and the correct answers: 
• MiniLM (paraphrase-MiniLM-L6-v2): is a lightweight 
yet powerful refined version of large transformer models 
like BERT and RoBERTa, designed for efficiency 
without compromising performance. By leveraging deep 
self-attention distillation, it effectively compresses the 
knowledge of larger models into a more compact form 
while preserving their core capabilities [32]. By 
optimizing the number of attention heads and hidden 
layers, MiniLM achieves fast inference speeds and 
efficient memory usage, making it ideal for real-time 
NLP applications. 
• BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from 
Transformers) 
(bert-base-nli-mean-tokens): 
is 
a 
transformer-based model developed by Google AI that 
revolutionized natural language processing. Unlike 
traditional models that process text sequentially, BERT 
leverages deep bidirectional representations, allowing it 
to jointly consider both left and right context across all 
layers [33]. This bidirectional approach enables a more 
comprehensive understanding of language nuances, 
making BERT highly effective for tasks such as question 
answering, sentiment analysis, and text classification. 
• RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining 
Approach) (sentence-transformers/all-roberta-large-v1): 
is an advanced refinement of BERT, developed by 
Facebook AI [34], that enhances performance through 
key optimizations. By training on larger mini-batches, 
eliminating the next-sentence prediction objective, and 
employing dynamic masking, RoBERTa achieves a 
deeper understanding of contextual relationships in text 
[34]. These improvements enable it to outperform BERT 
across various natural language processing tasks by 
capturing richer linguistic patterns while maintaining 
greater efficiency [35], [36], [37]. 
• Sentence-BERT (SBERT) (all-MiniLM-L12-v2): is an 
enhanced variant of BERT designed to generate 
semantically rich sentence embeddings using Siamese 
and triplet network architectures [38]. This approach not 
only captures contextual meaning more effectively but 
also drastically improves efficiency in tasks such as 
semantic similarity search and clustering. Unlike the

D. Grading Framework 
A scoring rubric was established based on similarity 
thresholds: 
• 
Similarity ≥ 0.8 → Score: 5 (Very similar) 
• 
0.6 ≤ Similarity < 0.8 → Score: 3 (Moderately similar) 
• 
0.4 ≤ Similarity < 0.6 → Score: 1 (Somewhat similar) 
• 
Similarity < 0.4 → Score: 0 (Not similar) 
Each model assigns a score to every student response, and 
total scores are computed for individual models. 
E. Evaluation Metrics 
To compare the effectiveness and reliability of different 
models, multiple evaluation metrics were applied: 
• Pearson and Spearman Correlation: Measures the 
strength of linear and rank-order agreement between 
model scores. 
• Cohen’s Kappa: Assesses inter-model agreement to 
determine consistency. 
• Score Distribution Analysis: Uses Kernel Density 
Estimation (KDE) to visualize scoring patterns across 
models. 
• Semantic Sensitivity Analysis: Evaluates how stable the 
models are when grading paraphrased responses. To 
assess the robustness of models to paraphrasing, a 
semantic sensitivity test was conducted. A set of 
paraphrased responses was created for three key 
questions while preserving their original meaning. Each 
model computed semantic similarity scores between the 
paraphrased responses and the corresponding correct 
answers. Higher scores indicate greater stability in 
recognizing reworded but semantically equivalent 
answers. This analysis evaluates the model’s ability to 
generalize beyond exact phrasing, ensuring consistency 
in grading. 
• Processing Time Analysis: Measures computational 
efficiency of each model. 
IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
The evaluation of the four transformer-based models 
(MiniLM, BERT, RoBERTa, and SBERT) for grading student 
responses revealed significant variations in their performance. 
Table I presents the Pearson correlation, Spearman correlation, 
and Cohen’s Kappa scores, which measure the similarity of total 
scores assigned by different models. 
Pearson and Spearman correlations indicate the degree of 
linear and rank-based agreement between models. The highest 
correlation values were observed between MiniLM and SBERT 
(Pearson: 0.909, Spearman: 0.908) (Table I), suggesting that 
these two models assigned highly similar scores. RoBERTa and 
SBERT also exhibited strong correlation (Pearson: 0.799, 
Spearman: 0.795), indicating comparable scoring patterns. In 
contrast, the lowest correlation was observed between BERT 
and RoBERTa (Pearson: 0.720, Spearman: 0.689), suggesting 
that these models differed substantially in their scoring 
approach. The strong correlation between SBERT and MiniLM 
supports the inclination that SBERT effectively captures 
semantic similarity, likely due to its specialized sentence 
representation training.

V. CONCLUSION 
This study demonstrated the effectiveness of transformer-
based models in automating the grading of open-ended 
questions, 
addressing 
key 
challenges 
of 
subjectivity, 
inconsistency, and inefficiency in manual assessment. By 
evaluating MiniLM, BERT, RoBERTa, and SBERT on multiple 
grading metrics, SBERT was identified as the most robust model 
for capturing semantic similarity with high sensitivity and strong 
correlation to human grading. MiniLM, while slightly less 
sensitive, provides a practical trade-off between efficiency and 
accuracy, making it a viable option for large-scale applications. 
The comparative analysis highlights the significant 
variations in model performance, particularly in agreement and 
computational efficiency. RoBERTa, despite its complexity, 
exhibits the highest processing time and variance in score 
distribution, making it less suitable for real-time grading. 
Meanwhile, the poor agreement between BERT and RoBERTa 
underscores the need for careful model selection to ensure 
reliable and consistent automated scoring. 
These findings underscore the transformative potential of 
AI-driven grading systems in educational settings. By 
leveraging transformer-based models like SBERT and MiniLM, 
institutions can reduce the manual grading burden, enhance 
grading consistency, and improve the fairness of student 
assessments. Future work will explore how human-expert 
evaluations could be incorporated to benchmark automated 
scoring against human grading, further validating model 
effectiveness. Fine-tuning of models and testing on larger 
datasets are also significant areas to explore in future work.
